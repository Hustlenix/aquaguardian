# How I Built an Entire Ocean Inside a Browser (And a Robot to Clean It)

*AquaGuardian — a devlog about making a website feel like a place, not a page.*

---

## What I'm working on

I built a website. But I didn't want it to feel like a website.

I wanted it to feel like diving.

AquaGuardian is a landing page where the background isn't an image and it isn't a video — it's a living 3D ocean, rendered in real time inside the browser, that reacts to your scroll. You start at the surface with light shafts cutting through the water. You scroll, and you descend. The lighting changes, the water gets deeper, fish drift past, coral sways, caustic light dances on the seabed. In the middle of all that sits the star of the show: a little guardian robot that surveys the ocean floor, sweeping scan beams across the sand, collecting debris, glowing cyan and gold.

It's live at [hustlenix.github.io/aquaguardian](https://hustlenix.github.io/aquaguardian/) — open it on a desktop, scroll slowly, and you'll see what I mean.

---

## Why I made this

The honest answer: because I was bored of the internet.

Every product page I saw was the same three boxes — hero, features, pricing — over a static gradient. Nobody remembered those pages. I kept thinking: *why does a website have to sit still?* We have WebGL in every browser. We have GPUs in every laptop. And yet the most ambitious thing most pages do is a fade-in.

So I set a rule for myself: **the background would be the product.** No static hero image. No video file. A real 3D scene, running at 60fps, that tells the story by *existing* while you read.

And once I had that rule, I needed a story worth the effort. I've always loved the ocean — it's the last unexplored frontier that sits right next to us, being quietly destroyed. So the narrative wrote itself: an autonomous robot that patrols the reef, picks up plastic, measures water quality, and reports its impact. It's optimistic sci-fi about conservation. Not a documentary — a dream of what's possible.

## The journey, in honest order

I've never built anything like this before. Here's how it actually went, with real milestones and real failures.

**Phase 1 — "Can I even put an ocean in a browser?" (Feb 2026)**

My first commit was literally "Build scene: ocean gradient, floating particles, coral." I didn't know Three.js. I did not know shaders. I wrote my first vertex shader by copying the fog example from the docs and praying.

It looked... fine. A blue plane, some dots, some coral blobs. The kind of scene you make when you're following a tutorial. I shipped it anyway — first versions of things should be embarrassing, that's how you know they're real.

**Phase 2 — The robot shows up (March 2026)**

Somewhere in the second month, I decided the scene needed a character. I built the robot out of primitive shapes — a cylinder body, an octahedron head, glowing eyes, a chest core, little antenna. No models, no downloads. Just math. That's the thing I'm most proud of: the entire world is built from `BoxGeometry` and `SphereGeometry` and custom GLSL. There is not one `.glb` file in the repo.

The robot changed everything. Once you have a character, people stop asking "what is this?" and start asking "what is it doing?"

**Phase 3 — The scroll becomes the story (March–April 2026)**

The turning point was wiring scroll position to the scene. Each section of the page drives a "scene state" — camera height, fog density, lighting color, fish behavior, robot activity. Scroll past the "crisis" section and the water gets murkier. Reach the "robot reveal" and the scan beams switch on. The story isn't told *over* the scene — the scene *is* the story.

I rewrote the camera system three times. The first was a hard cut between sections — jarring. The second lerped positions but felt robotic. The third, which shipped, uses per-section easing speeds, field-of-view animation, and mouse parallax. When it works, you don't notice it. That's the point.

**Phase 4 — The painful honesty pass (August 2026)**

Here's the part I have to be honest about, because the whole point of this log is honesty: **I originally wrote fake stats.**

"8 million tons of plastic." "90% of species affected." Numbers I pulled from the air and formatted beautifully with CSS. And you know what? Nobody would have known. They looked *great*.

But it was fake. And the thing I was building was a story about *caring* about something real. The moment I realized the contradiction, I did two full cleanup passes — the git history literally reads "Remove remaining fabricated claims" twice. I replaced the invented numbers with a generated demo dataset, clearly labeled as demo data, generated by a native C tool I wrote so the numbers are reproducible. The real story is strong enough. It didn't need lies.

**Phase 5 — Making it beautiful (August 2026)**

The final push was cinematic polish. I discovered my postprocessing and caustics were implemented but never actually rendered — the scene had been shipping without bloom this whole time. Flipping that on was like taking off sunglasses in sunlight.

Now the scene runs: layered multi-octave waves with white glints, volumetric god rays with true radial falloff, animated caustics drifting gold across the seabed, film grain, vignette, chromatic aberration. Particles twinkle on two harmonics like plankton. Bubbles wobble and pop. Fish schools — 40 of them at high quality — bank and turn together. The robot hovers, its antenna swaying, chest core pulsing when it's active.

## The tech, briefly

- **Next.js 15 + React 19 + TypeScript** — the shell. Static export, deployed to GitHub Pages.
- **Three.js + React Three Fiber + custom GLSL shaders** — the ocean, caustics, and light shafts are all computed on the GPU. No textures were downloaded; everything is procedural.
- **Zustand** — one store drives both the story and the world. Scene components subscribe narrowly to the slices they read, so scroll updates don't re-render the whole canvas.
- **Framer Motion + GSAP + Lenis** — the UI animations and buttery smooth scroll.
- **Tailwind CSS 4** — with a full design-token system for colors, type, motion, and elevation.
- **Quality tiers** — mobile gets reduced geometry, no postprocessing chain. Desktop gets the full cinematic grade. Nobody should scroll a laggy page.

42 commits. 6 months. One ocean.

## What I learned

1. **The scrollbar is a design element.** On this site, it's a thin gold line. Small details tell people a site was cared about.
2. **Restraint is the hard part.** I added and removed more features than shipped. Every "cool idea" that made the scene noisy got cut.
3. **Honesty is a feature.** The fake-stats moment was my lowest point and the fix became one of the best parts of the project's story.
4. **Primitives are liberating.** No asset pipeline, no model downloads — just math. It forced me to learn what the GPU actually does.
5. **Performance is a feature, not a chore.** The quality tiers aren't an afterthought; they're what let me add the heavy stuff without shame.

## What's next

The homepage is the showcase. Behind it, there's already a small ecosystem — an impact dashboard, a mission tracker, community challenges, a learning section, an AI assistant page, and a mobile companion view. They share one design system and one data pipeline. Next up: deepening the story chapters, and maybe letting the robot actually *do* more things on screen.
